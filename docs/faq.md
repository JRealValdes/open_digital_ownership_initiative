# Frequently asked questions

## Is this a law, a standard, or a manifesto?

It is a **community design proposal**. It aims to be precise enough to
guide standards and product design, but it is not legislation and not yet
a formal industry standard.

## Does this ban subscriptions?

No. Subscriptions and rentals can exist alongside purchases. The
framework objects to **purchase-priced** products that only grant thin,
revocable permission while being presented as buying a game.

## Is Offline Ownership just DRM hardware?

Not necessarily. The requirement is a **unique, non-duplicable proof of
ownership** that can be held physically and used without depending on a
store for everyday play. Secure hardware is one possible implementation;
the framework does not mandate a vendor or chip.

## Can I still pirate / does this stop piracy?

This proposal is about **rights for paying customers**, not about making
infringement impossible. Perfect technical prevention of all abuse is not
a goal. The security posture is to stop *ownership duplication* and
large-scale fraud while trusting ordinary owners.

## What happens when a platform shuts down?

The model requires that ownership be designed to survive a single
storefront: exportable records, offline tokens, and transferability.
No document can force defunct servers to stay online; the design goal is
that access and proof of ownership are not trapped inside one company’s
convenience layer.

## How do old discs and cartridges fit?

In principle, physical editions can act as—or be paired with—ownership
tokens in Offline mode. How legacy stock is bridged into the unified
library is an open adoption question (see framework §16).

## Who is supposed to implement this?

Platforms, publishers, retailers, standards bodies, and—indirectly—
regulators and consumer advocates. The repository exists so the model can
be improved in the open before or alongside any formal standardisation.

## Does this require games to work across PlayStation, Xbox, PC, etc.?

No. Multi-device use **within** one platform is in scope. Moving the same
owned copy between competing ecosystems is explicitly out of scope for
this version—it is not required for the ownership rights the framework
defines.

## Why start with video games only?

Games combine high prices, platform lock-in, strong physical traditions,
and rapid digitisation—so ownership tensions are especially visible. The
principles may later extend to other digital goods via RFCs.
